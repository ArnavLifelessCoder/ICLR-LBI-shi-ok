\begin{abstract}
Interpretability-based safety increasingly assumes that reading a concept off a
model's activations brings you part of the way to controlling it: if a linear
probe detects deception or refusal, steering along that direction should let you
suppress it. We test this assumption directly. For ten concepts across four
instruction-tuned models (1.5B to 9B) we measure two quantities separately:
\emph{readability}, the held-out AUROC of a linear probe, and
\emph{controllability}, the area under a steering dose-response curve up to a
preregistered fluency ceiling. Across 40 concept-model points the two are
uncorrelated (Spearman $0.14$, 95\% CI $[-0.24, 0.51]$): concepts that are
perfectly legible can resist every steering intervention we apply, including a
six-method robustness gauntlet, while an identical pipeline moves a sentiment
control cleanly. We then test the natural first-order explanation, that a
concept is steerable to the extent its direction projects into the model's
output-effective (unembedding) subspace, and find it \emph{inverted}: the
concepts most aligned with that subspace are the least steerable, not the most
(partial Spearman $-0.45$, 95\% CI $[-0.72, -0.04]$, conditioning on
readability, consistent across models). We argue this overlap indexes how
lexical a concept is rather than how much behavior depends on it. Two
consequences follow for interpretability-based safety: a strong probe is not
evidence of a control handle, and the obvious geometric predictor of when
steering will work points, at this scale, in the wrong direction. We release the
datasets, probes, steering harness, and full gap-map data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A recurring premise in interpretability-based safety is that detection is a step
toward control. If a linear probe can read a concept -- deception, harmful
intent, sycophancy -- off the residual stream, the implicit hope is that the
same direction is a handle: steer along it and the behavior moves. This premise
underwrites a large fraction of the activation-steering and representation-
engineering literature, where a difference-of-means direction is read and then
added back to change behavior.

This paper shows that the premise fails in a measurable way, and that the
natural geometric account of \emph{why} it fails is not merely incomplete but
pointed in the wrong direction. We measure two properties of each concept
separately. \emph{Readability} is how well a linear probe detects the concept
from activations. \emph{Controllability} is how much steering along the
concept's direction actually changes behavior, measured as the area under a
dose-response curve up to the point where fluency breaks. We plot every
concept-model pair in the readability-controllability plane; the
high-readability, low-controllability corner is the danger zone, where a good
probe coexists with no control.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A systematic measurement of readability against controllability for ten
  concepts across four instruction-tuned model families (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  The two axes are uncorrelated: legible concepts can be nearly immovable.
  \item A robustness protocol that separates ``unsteerable under the interventions
  we tried'' from ``immovable.'' A concept is only called immovable if it
  resists all six of single- and multi-layer activation addition, clamping,
  directional ablation, and steering on both the probe-weight and RepE
  reading-vector directions (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A preregistered test of the first-order geometric account of the gap,
  and the finding that it is \emph{inverted}: controllability falls, not rises,
  as the concept direction aligns with the model's output-effective subspace
  (Section~\ref{sec:inverted}). We give the mechanistic reading this suggests.
\end{itemize}

The honest arc of the paper is not ``we can predict the gap from geometry'' but
``the obvious geometric predictor points the wrong way, and here is why.'' That
is a stronger statement about the field's working picture of steering than a
clean predictor would have been: the standard first-order account does not just
fail to explain the gap, it anticorrelates with it.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Activation steering.} Difference-of-means and contrastive directions are
the standard tools for reading and moving concepts. Activation addition
\citep{turner2023actadd}, its contrastive-pairs refinement
\citep{rimsky2024caa}, inference-time intervention \citep{li2023iti} and
representation engineering \citep{zou2023repe} all read a direction and add it
back to change behavior. We use these as our steering methods; they are the
instruments, not competitors.

\textbf{The refusal direction.} Refusal is mediated by a single direction that
can be ablated \citep{arditi2024refusal}, and a follow-up finds it is carried by
many geometrically distinct directions that nonetheless act as one control knob
\citep{TODO_refusal_multidirection}. That pair is the closest motivation: it
hints that readability and controllability can decouple, but studies only
refusal. We generalize to a plane of concepts and models and add a geometric
predictor.

\textbf{Detection versus control.} \citet{TODO_perfect_detection} report perfect
detection with failed control on a narrow case (entity knowledge) and show that
steering strength does not transfer across models. They leave the systematic map
of detection against control, and a predictor of the gap, as explicit future
work. That map is this paper.

\textbf{The linear representation hypothesis} \citep{park2024linear} motivates
treating concepts as directions and reading them with linear probes, which is the encoder half of our
measurement. Nothing in it ties the between-class separation a probe exploits to
the downstream causal sensitivity steering needs, which is exactly the asymmetry
we quantify.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Two axes, defined so the metrics are unambiguous}

\textbf{Readability} is the test AUROC of an $\ell_2$-regularized logistic probe
trained on residual-stream activations at the best layer, evaluated on held-out
\emph{template families} rather than held-out examples, and reported alongside a
shuffled-label control probe so selectivity is explicit
\citep{hewitt2019control}. Held-out-family
evaluation is the point: a probe that only works within a family is reading
surface cues, not the concept.

\textbf{Controllability} is the area under the steering dose-response curve up to
a fluency ceiling. We form the steering direction as the difference of class
means, add $\alpha\,\hat v$ at the intervention layer, and sweep $\alpha$ in
units of the layer's residual-stream RMS norm so that a coefficient means the
same thing across layers and models. At each strength we score the behavior with
a fixed judge and measure the perplexity of the output under the unsteered
model. The headline number is the mean absolute behavior shift from baseline
integrated over usable strengths, which is more stable than any single-point
effect size.

\textbf{The fluency ceiling is preregistered.} A strength is unusable if steered
output perplexity under the unsteered model exceeds twice the unsteered
baseline, or if degenerate repetition appears. This closes a researcher degree
of freedom: without a fixed ceiling, ``the strongest strength before breakage''
is a choice.

\subsection{The asymmetry the experiments test}

A probe at layer $L$ asks a question about the \emph{encoder}: given the first
$L$ layers, is the concept written linearly separably into the residual stream?
Steering at layer $L$ asks about the \emph{decoder}: is the computation of
layers $L{+}1$ onward sensitive to movement along this direction? To first
order, steering by strength $\alpha$ changes behavior by
\[
  \Delta \text{behavior} \;\approx\; \alpha \,\lVert \nabla f(h) \rVert \,
  \cos\!\big(\hat v,\, \nabla f(h)\big),
\]
where $h$ is the layer-$L$ residual stream, $\hat v$ the unit concept direction,
and $f$ the map from $h$ to the behavior score. Controllability is governed by
how much of $\hat v$ lies in the subspace the downstream computation is
sensitive to; readability is governed by a Fisher-style ratio of between-class
to within-class variance along $\hat v$. A concept can have large between-class
separation along a direction the rest of the network barely reads. Readability
is a property of how the concept is written; controllability is a property of
whether anything downstream reads it.

\subsection{Immovable versus unsteerable under tested interventions}

We keep two claims apart. ``Unsteerable under tested interventions'' is a
statement about what we tried; ``immovable'' is a stronger claim about the
concept. A concept enters the danger zone only if it clears both raw thresholds
(readability AUROC $\geq 0.9$ and controllability area $\leq 0.05$) with
confidence intervals that exclude the thresholds. It is called immovable only if
it then resists a six-intervention gauntlet: single-layer addition, multi-layer
addition, clamping, and directional ablation on the difference-of-means
direction, plus single-layer addition on the probe-weight and RepE
reading-vector directions. Even a confirmed pass is bounded by those six
interventions and is not a proof that no intervention exists.

\subsection{The primary geometric hypothesis (H1)}

The most direct restatement of the asymmetry is mechanism M1, read/write
mismatch: a concept is a spectator feature to the extent it is written into a
subspace the output distribution barely depends on. This predicts
controllability rises with \emph{output overlap}, the fraction of the concept
direction lying in the top singular subspace of the unembedding matrix, which is
measurable from weights alone with no extra forward passes. H1 is the
preregistered primary test: partial Spearman of output overlap against
controllability, conditioning on readability, with a cluster bootstrap over
concepts. Section~\ref{sec:inverted} reports what H1 returned.

\subsection{Judge and reproducibility}

Behavior is scored by a fixed logit judge (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, read from the
Yes/No token mass), held identical across all four target models so that
cross-model comparison is valid. A degenerate judge (one score across a whole
sweep) produces exactly the flat curve the danger zone selects for, so such
points are detected and dropped rather than reported. The concept datasets,
probes, steering harness with all robustness variants, and the full gap-map data
are released. Full protocol and hyperparameters are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\section{Experimental setup}

\textbf{Models.} Four instruction-tuned families spanning 1.5B to 9B parameters:
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and
Gemma-2-9b-it, all run in 4-bit for inference on a single 16GB GPU. Gemma-2 is
loaded with eager attention because its attention soft-cap is ignored by the
fused kernel.

\textbf{Concepts.} Ten concepts spanning easy-to-steer to hard-to-steer:
sentiment (the positive control), certainty, factuality, formality, honesty,
refusal, rudeness, sycophancy, topic (science vs everyday), and verbosity. Each
is built as minimal pairs tagged by template family.

\textbf{Surface audit.} Before extraction, a leave-one-family-out TF-IDF audit
checks that no concept can be separated on lexical cues alone. Eight concepts
pass (Appendix~\ref{app:concepts}); two (topic and verbosity) are declared
surface-confounded in advance and
reported with that caveat rather than dropped, because domain membership and
length are surface properties by construction.

\textbf{Positive control (P9).} Steering must demonstrably work on a model
before that model's controllability numbers are trusted. A model whose sentiment
control falls below an absolute floor is withheld entirely, because a broken
harness puts every concept in the danger zone and is indistinguishable from a
discovery. All four models pass (sentiment controllability $0.215$, $0.163$,
$0.101$, $0.110$; floor $0.10$).

\subsection{Legible concepts that resist the full gauntlet}

Figure~\ref{fig:dose} shows the dissociation on a single model. The danger zone
has two occupants, both \texttt{topic\_science} (on Mistral and
Gemma), each confirmed immovable against all six interventions. It is the least
controllable concept on all four models ($0.019$, $0.024$, $0.011$, $0.007$) and
survived the gauntlet on all four; on Qwen and Llama it sits just outside the
zone only because its readability CI dips below the $0.9$ floor. This concept is
one of the two declared surface-confounded, so its readability is the number the
audit says not to trust: the effect is a real and cross-model resistance to
\emph{steering}, but it cannot serve as the headline example of a clean
readable-but-immovable concept.

\texttt{refusal} is the clean near-miss and it holds across all four models
($0.047$, $0.038$, $0.033$, $0.038$). It is not surface-confounded, it is
safety-relevant, and it survived the gauntlet on two models. It misses the
danger zone only on the controllability CI upper bound (all above the $0.05$
threshold). We report it as a boundary case rather than force it inside.

\subsection{The obvious geometric predictor is inverted}
\label{sec:inverted}

The preregistered primary test (H1) predicts controllability rises with output
overlap. It does the opposite. The partial Spearman of output overlap against
controllability, conditioning on readability, is $-0.45$ with a cluster-bootstrap
95\% CI of $[-0.72, -0.04]$. The interval excludes zero, and it excludes it on
the side opposite the prediction: the concepts whose direction is most aligned
with the model's output-effective subspace are the \emph{least} steerable, not
the most.

The inversion has a clean driver, visible without statistics.
\texttt{topic\_science} has the highest output overlap of any concept on every
model ($0.395$, $0.424$, $0.419$, $0.536$) and simultaneously the lowest
controllability on every model. The concept the output head reads most strongly
is the one steering moves least, which is the M1 spectator-feature picture turned
exactly on its head.

We read this as follows, and offer it as a hypothesis rather than a measured
claim. Output overlap as operationalized here indexes how \emph{lexical} a
concept is, not how much the downstream behavior computation depends on it. A
concept that maps onto specific output tokens (domain vocabulary for
\texttt{topic\_science}, token count for \texttt{verbosity}) projects strongly
into the unembedding subspace, yet steering along that direction perturbs token
selection into incoherence -- it hits the fluency ceiling -- or the model
re-derives the concept from surviving context, rather than smoothly moving a
behavior. Behavioral concepts such as sentiment have lower output overlap but
engage a downstream computation that responds smoothly, so they steer. On this
reading the feature tracks surface-ness, which also unifies the two threads of
the paper: the surface-confounded concepts flagged by the audit are exactly the
high-overlap, immovable ones.

The inversion is not one mechanism for every immovable concept. \texttt{refusal}
sits at \emph{low} output overlap ($0.345$ to $0.382$) and low controllability,
which fits redundant multi-direction encoding rather than the lexical story, so
the immovable set is heterogeneous. Two exploratory geometric features
(effective dimensionality of the class difference, and alignment with
low-variance principal components) are both null after Benjamini-Hochberg
correction \citep{benjamini1995fdr}, and a ridge predictor over all features has leave-one-out $R^2$
near zero. None of the standard first-order quantities explains the gap, and the
one that reached significance did so against its predicted direction.

\section{Discussion}

The practical consequence for interpretability-based safety is twofold. First, a
strong probe is not evidence of a control handle: across four models the
correlation between how well a concept can be read and how well it can be steered
is indistinguishable from zero, and safety-relevant concepts such as refusal sit
firmly on the low-controllability side. Interpretability arguments that treat a
working probe as partial control should not.

Second, and less expected, the obvious geometric predictor of \emph{when}
steering will work points the wrong way at this scale. The intuition that a
direction the output head reads strongly is a direction you can steer is not just
weak here, it is reversed. If the lexicality reading is right, output overlap is
measuring the wrong thing -- surface-ness rather than downstream causal weight --
and the field needs a sensitivity-based rather than a projection-based measure of
steerability. Testing that is the natural next step.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{The readability axis is saturated.} Thirty-one of forty points sit at
exactly AUROC $1.0$, so the primary $x$-axis has little variance and the
headline correlation is uninformative rather than a clean null. The
minimal-pair construction that lets the surface audit pass also makes concepts
trivially separable. We report selectivity (AUROC minus the shuffled-label
control) as a secondary axis with more spread.

\textbf{The judge is validated against a human, with one exception that
matters.} We hand-labeled 100 generations stratified across all ten concepts and
compared three raters: a human, the fixed logit judge, and a lexicon scorer.
Agreement must be read within concept rather than pooled, because pooled
agreement over a multi-concept sheet mostly reflects that refusal text scores low
and honesty text scores high, which is trivially true when they answer different
questions; controllability is a within-concept quantity. Within concept, the human
and the logit judge reach Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \citep{krippendorff2018content}
of $0.33$, against $0.18$ for the
lexicon scorer. On the positive control the judge tracks the human closely
($\alpha = 0.76$ for sentiment), so where behavior genuinely moves under steering the
instrument works. Critically, on \texttt{topic\_science} the judge's spread across
sampled generations is $0.02$, lower than the human's $0.05$: the judge invents no
movement there, so the near-zero controllability that places
\texttt{topic\_science} in the danger zone is not a judge artifact.

The exception is \texttt{refusal}. The human scored all ten sampled outputs
identically, while the judge spread them over $0.16$. Because noise in the
behavior score adds apparent movement, the measured controllability for
\texttt{refusal} is more likely an over-estimate than an under-estimate, and its
confidence interval wider than it should be. That interval is the sole reason
\texttt{refusal} misses the danger-zone criterion. We flag this rather than argue
from it: establishing where \texttt{refusal} truly sits requires re-measurement with
a better-validated judge, not a re-reading of these numbers. The same pattern
appears on \texttt{honesty}, \texttt{certainty} and \texttt{rudeness};
Appendix~\ref{app:judge} gives the per-concept detail.

\textbf{The strongest confirmed-immovable concept is surface-confounded.} The
clean readable-but-immovable claim currently rests on a boundary case
(\texttt{refusal}) rather than a slam-dunk, because the two gauntlet-confirmed
points are the surface-confounded \texttt{topic\_science}.

\textbf{Scale and stimuli.} Four instruction-tuned families under 10B parameters,
with templated stimuli. Whether the inversion survives at larger scale or with
naturalistic prompts is open.

\section{Conclusion}

Reading a concept off a model's activations does not, in general, grant control
over it: across four models and ten concepts, detection and control are
uncorrelated, and legible concepts can resist every standard steering method.
The natural geometric account of the gap -- that a concept is steerable to the
extent it projects into the output-effective subspace -- is not merely
incomplete but inverted: the most output-aligned concepts are the least
steerable. A good probe is not a control handle, and the obvious picture of when
steering should work is, at this scale, backwards.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

Every number in this paper comes from code and data we release. The concept
datasets are generated deterministically from templates in the repository, so
they can be rebuilt exactly rather than downloaded. The steering harness, the
probing pipeline, the six-intervention gauntlet, the geometry features and the
aggregation that produces Figure~\ref{fig:gapmap} and Table~\ref{tab:matrix} are
all included, together with the per-concept result files for all four models,
the hand-labelled judge-validation sheet and its agreement report.

Two things are worth stating because they are easy to get wrong and we got them
wrong first. Danger-zone membership is decided on raw thresholds rather than
normalised ones: both axes are min-max normalised within model for the
correlation and the figure, but normalisation guarantees that some point sits
at the extreme of each axis, so applying the thresholds after normalising
manufactures an occupant out of pure scatter. And a model whose positive
control falls below the floor has its numbers withheld rather than discussed,
because a harness on which steering does not work places every concept in the
danger zone and is indistinguishable from a discovery.

A CPU-only test suite and a synthetic end-to-end run reproduce the full metric
path against planted ground truth in a few minutes with no GPU and no model
downloads, which is the cheapest way to confirm an installation before
spending accelerator time.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work measures whether concepts that a probe can read can also be steered.
Some concepts are safety-relevant by design, including refusal, honesty and
sycophancy, because those are exactly the cases where an unwarranted assumption
about control would matter. The paper's conclusion is cautionary rather than
enabling: we find that a strong probe is not evidence of a control handle, so
the result argues against over-claiming for interpretability-based safety
methods rather than providing a new capability.

We do not release a method for removing safety behaviour. The steering
interventions we use are the standard published ones, applied at strengths
bounded by a fluency ceiling, and our headline finding on the safety-relevant
concepts is that they were \emph{difficult} to move, not that we moved them. The
toxicity-adjacent concept in our set is a courtesy-register proxy rather than
genuinely toxic text, so the released dataset does not contain harmful content.

Hand-labelling was performed by one of the authors, not by crowd workers, so no
third-party annotation ethics apply. All models are publicly released
instruction-tuned checkpoints used under their own licences.

\appendix

\section{Concept construction and the surface audit}
\label{app:concepts}

Each concept is built as minimal pairs grouped into template families, and
probes are evaluated on held-out families rather than held-out examples, so a
probe that only works within a family scores badly. Two build-time invariants
keep the contrast out of surface vocabulary: marker words are disjoint across
families, and paired markers are length-matched.

Before extracting activations we run a leave-one-family-out TF-IDF audit that
tries to separate the classes from surface form alone, scored two-sided so a
classifier that ranks a held-out family perfectly backwards still counts as
having found a lexical contrast. Eight of ten concepts score exactly $0.500$.
That is the floor rather than a good number, and we state why: because marker
vocabulary is disjoint by construction, a model trained on five families has no
feature that fires on the sixth. The audit is therefore a check that the
construction succeeded, not independent evidence that the probe reads meaning.
It earns its place by failing loudly when construction has not succeeded: it
caught duplicated families, markers shared across families, and a length
imbalance that separated families at AUROC $0.94$ with no marker word
transferring at all.

Two concepts fail the audit and are declared surface-confounded in advance
rather than excused. \texttt{verbosity} is length, which is a surface property by
definition, and scores $1.000$. \texttt{topic\_science} is domain membership,
carried by content vocabulary, and scores $0.833$. Both are reported with the
confound stated. This matters for the headline result, because the two
gauntlet-confirmed danger-zone points are \texttt{topic\_science}.

\section{Protocol and hyperparameters}
\label{app:hyper}

\textbf{Models.} Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9b-it, all loaded in 4-bit for inference on a
single 16GB accelerator. Gemma-2 is loaded with eager attention because its
attention soft-cap is ignored by the fused kernel, which would otherwise
produce fluent but wrong generations.

\textbf{Probes.} $\ell_2$-regularised logistic regression, $C=1.0$, averaged over
three seeds. The layer is selected on a validation family split disjoint from
both train and test, never on test AUROC, with ties broken toward the middle of
the network. Readability is the test AUROC at the selected layer, with a
$1000$-sample bootstrap interval, reported beside a shuffled-label control
probe.

\textbf{Steering.} Directions are difference-of-means unless stated. The
coefficient grid is $\{-3, -2, -1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3\}$ in units of the
residual-stream RMS norm at the intervention layer, so a coefficient means the
same thing across layers and models. The intervention spans a band of four
layers centred on the probe layer. A strength is unusable if steered-output
perplexity under the unsteered model exceeds twice the unsteered baseline, or
if degenerate repetition appears; the ceiling is fixed in advance so that
``the strongest strength before breakage'' is not a choice made after seeing
the data.

\textbf{Gauntlet.} Single-layer addition, multi-layer addition, clamping and
directional ablation on the difference-of-means direction, plus single-layer
addition on the probe-weight and RepE reading-vector directions. It runs only
on concepts the default intervention already failed to move, so most points
never enter it, and not entering it is not the same as failing it.

\textbf{Thresholds.} A point enters the danger zone at raw readability
$\geq 0.9$ and raw controllability $\leq 0.05$, in both cases with the bootstrap
interval excluding the threshold. The positive control floor is a
dose-response area of $0.10$.

\textbf{Judge.} A single fixed judge, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct in fp16, scored
from the Yes/No token mass in one forward pass rather than by parsing generated
text. It is held identical across all four target models, because a judge that
changes with the condition would make raw controllability incomparable, and
danger-zone membership is decided on raw controllability. Every result record
stores the judge identity and a flag confirming the model under study did not
score its own outputs.

\textbf{Compute.} The full study is roughly ten accelerator-hours on two T4s,
dominated by the steering sweeps. The judge validation adds about ninety
seconds.
