\begin{abstract}
Activation steering is usually evaluated by adding a concept direction to the
residual stream and reporting how much behavior changes. That summary cannot
tell steering a model in the intended direction from perturbing it, because it
integrates the absolute deviation from baseline and so counts both. We propose
the obvious repair, integrating the signed deviation so that an intervention is
credited only when positive coefficients raise the target behavior and negative
ones lower it, and we validate it on concepts where the correct answer is known
in advance.

The validation is the contribution. We build ten concepts whose behavioral
readout is a rule over the generated string rather than a judgment, so no model
scores the outcome and the intended direction follows from construction. They
are chosen so that baseline position varies independently of concept identity,
spanning $0.00$ to $0.98$, which is what separates having room to move from
being the kind of concept that moves. Across four instruction-tuned models, 26
of 40 points have a signed area whose interval excludes zero and 25 of those are
positive, the direction the concept was built to be steered in. Three concepts
resolve on all four models. One point resolves the other way, \texttt{gt\_question}
on Llama ($-0.024$, Spearman $-0.96$ in coefficient), and we report it as a
reversal rather than noise.

The check therefore detects real steering when real steering is present, which
is what licenses reading its nulls as statements about concepts rather than
about the instrument. It also rules out the obvious confound: whether a sign
resolves is unrelated to how much headroom the concept has (Spearman $+0.01$,
$p=0.97$), and the room available to an absolute deviation correlates
\emph{negatively} with the measured effect ($-0.52$, $p=0.001$), the reverse of
what a ceiling artifact predicts.

With that established, the same measurement on judge-scored concepts behaves
differently, and the difference is the judge. Where a rule computes the readout,
the absolute and signed summaries almost coincide, with a median $1.000$ of the
measured effect attributable to directional movement. Where an LLM judge
computes it, the median is $0.387$ and the two summaries are uncorrelated. On
the same four models with the same grid and prompt count, the rule readout
resolves 26 of 40 points and the judge 7 of 16. Enlarging the judge's evaluation
set fivefold does not rescue it: the intervals tighten as sampling predicts, but
the point estimates collapse toward zero and 7 of 16 signs flip, so the
small-sample estimates were not merely uncertain. Re-scoring identical sweeps
with a second judge moves absolute controllability by between $0.5\times$ and $26.8\times$ and flips 3 of 16 signs, so a published
controllability number is not comparable across judges. A danger-zone criterion
from our own preregistration selects one concept under the smaller judge and
none under the larger.

We then show the cost to a substantive result. We preregistered that a direction
is steerable to the extent it projects into the model's output-effective
subspace. Measured with the absolute summary the estimate is inverted (partial
Spearman $-0.45$), and it survives a fluency-ceiling control and
leave-one-concept-out. Measured with the signed summary it returns $+0.14$. A
result can pass the robustness checks the field asks for and still be a property
of its own summary statistic. We release the stimuli, the harness, the
per-coefficient curves, and the rule-scored concepts as a reusable check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Activation steering is evaluated by intervening on a model and measuring the
change in its behavior. A concept direction is estimated from contrastive
activations, added to the residual stream across a range of strengths, and the
resulting shift is summarized as a magnitude: how far the model moved. That
summary is silent on the question it appears to answer. An intervention that
displaces activations along an arbitrary direction will change what a model
says, and a metric recording the size of that change will score it. Only a
metric that tracks the sign can separate control from disturbance.

The repair is obvious once stated: integrate the signed deviation, so an
intervention is credited only when positive coefficients raise the target
behavior and negative ones lower it. The difficulty is that the repair cannot
justify itself. A signed summary returning nothing might mean the intervention
had no directional effect, or that the summary cannot see one, and separating
those needs a case where the answer is known independently. Steering research
rarely has one, because the behavioral readout is itself a model.

So we build one. Four concepts whose readout is a rule over the generated
string -- word count, capitalized fraction, digit fraction, French
function-word fraction -- have no judge in the loop and an intended direction
that follows from construction, so a signed area can be scored correct or
incorrect rather than merely reported. Across four instruction-tuned models, 5
of 16 points resolve and every one is positive; steering into French resolves on
three models and into capitals on two. Nothing in the study resolves against the
direction it was estimated from. This is the claim the rest depends on: the
check works where we can check it.

That licenses the second claim. Applying both summaries to judge-scored
concepts, they diverge, and the divergence tracks the instrument rather than the
intervention. With a rule computing the readout the median share of measured
effect that is directional is $1.000$; with an LLM judge it is $0.387$, and the
two summaries are uncorrelated. On matched models, grid and prompt count the
rule readout resolves 26 of 40 signs and the judge 7 of 16. Re-scoring identical sweeps under a second judge
moves absolute controllability by up to $26.8\times$ and flips 3 of 16 signs. A
controllability number reported without its judge is not a quantity another
paper can compare against.

The third claim is what this costs a conclusion. We preregistered a geometric
account of when steering should work and, measured with the absolute summary,
found it inverted. The inversion survives a fluency-ceiling control and survives
leave-one-concept-out. It does not survive changing the summary. We report the
whole sequence because the checks are the point: a result can pass the
diagnostics the field asks for and still be an artifact of the statistic
underneath it.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A directional check for steering evaluations, with intervals and an
  arm split, \emph{validated} against concepts whose correct answer is known by
  construction because their readout is a rule rather than a judgment
  (Section~\ref{sec:groundtruth}).
  \item Evidence that the absolute summary's divergence from the directional one
  is a property of LLM-judge scoring, not of steering, and that judge-scored
  magnitudes move by up to $26.8\times$ and signs by 3 in 16 across judges
  (Section~\ref{sec:judgedep}).
  \item A worked demonstration that a preregistered geometric result survives a
  ceiling control and leave-one-concept-out but not a change of metric
  (Section~\ref{sec:inverted}).
  \item A robustness protocol separating ``unsteerable under the interventions
  we tried'' from ``immovable'' (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and an account of
  what a saturated readability axis can and cannot establish.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reading concepts out of activations}

Linear probes on intermediate activations are a standard diagnostic for what a
network has computed by a given depth \citep{alain2016probes}, and what they
license has been contested for almost as long. \citet{hewitt2019control} show
that probe accuracy alone is uninterpretable, since a sufficiently expressive
probe can fit an arbitrary control task, and propose reporting selectivity; we
report a shuffled-label control for every concept. \citet{belinkov2022probing}
surveys the resulting literature.

The linear representation hypothesis \citep{park2024linear} justifies treating a
concept as a direction and underwrites difference-of-means estimation, but it is
a claim about how concepts are encoded. Nothing in it connects the between-class
separation a probe exploits to the downstream sensitivity steering requires.
Unsupervised methods recover directions without labelled contrasts
\citep{burns2023ccs, cunningham2024sae}; we use supervised contrasts throughout.

\subsection{From reading to intervening}

The closest methodological ancestor is amnesic probing
\citep{elazar2021amnesic}, which argues that probing accuracy does not establish
that a model uses the probed property, and tests use by removing the property and
measuring the consequence. The removal operation has its own literature, from
iterative nullspace projection \citep{ravfogel2020inlp} to the closed-form LEACE
\citep{belrose2023leace}; directional ablation, one of our six interventions, is
its single-direction case.

Our question is the mirror image: we add signal along a direction and sweep
strength rather than testing a single removal. The two can come apart, since a
concept the model genuinely uses may be immovable if the downstream computation
is insensitive to displacement along the direction a difference of means
recovers.

More broadly, editing activations to test what a component does is standard
practice, from causal tracing and rank-one weight editing
\citep{meng2022rome} onward. The activation-patching methodology literature
documents how strongly the reported conclusion depends on the metric and the
corruption chosen \citep{zhang2023patching}, which is one reason we fix the
dose-response metric and the fluency ceiling in advance rather than selecting
them after seeing results. Our interventions belong to this family but are
applied to a fixed concept direction rather than to a located component.

\subsection{Steering methods and their known limits}

Difference-of-means and contrastive directions are the standard tools for moving
concepts: activation addition \citep{turner2023actadd} and its contrastive-pairs
refinement \citep{panickssery2023caa}, inference-time intervention
\citep{li2023iti}, and representation engineering \citep{zou2023repe}. We use
these as our interventions rather than compare against them, since the paper
measures how far a concept moves under standard methods, not which method moves
it furthest.

Negative results already exist. \citet{tan2024steering} report that steerability
varies across inputs within a concept and that steering vectors are brittle to
reasonable prompt changes. That variation is within-concept; ours is across
concepts and paired with a detection measurement on the same concepts. The two
compound: a method unreliable across inputs and separately inert for some
concepts is weaker evidence for control than either result alone suggests.

\subsection{Directions that do carry causal weight}

Nothing here claims that concept directions never control behavior, and the
counterexamples calibrate the claim. \citet{marks2023truth} find that
difference-in-mean probes, the estimator we use, recover directions more causally
implicated in outputs than other probing methods, and \citet{todd2024function}
show a vector extracted from in-context demonstrations triggering the
demonstrated task in unrelated contexts. Both are existence proofs that a
direction read out of activations can be a control handle; our result is that
whether it is cannot be assumed from detection accuracy. Relatedly,
\citet{bolukbasi2021illusion} document an interpretability illusion in which
directions appear to encode a simple concept while encoding something else.
Their illusion concerns what a direction means, ours what it does, and both are
reasons to treat a high probe score as the start of an argument.

\subsection{Refusal, and the one prior detection-versus-control result}

Refusal is the most studied single concept in this literature.
\citet{arditi2024refusal} show that refusal is mediated by a direction whose
ablation prevents refusal across many chat models, which is the strongest
existing case that a concept direction is a control handle.
\citet{joad2026refusal} complicate this: refusal spans eleven categories on
geometrically distinct directions that nonetheless behave as a shared
one-dimensional control. That a concept can be carried redundantly is one
candidate explanation for resistance to intervention on any single direction,
and is why we read refusal's behavior in our data as consistent with redundant
encoding rather than with the lexical account we offer elsewhere.

\citet{galeone2026perfect} report the one prior result measuring detection
against control directly: on a hallucination case, fake entities are detected
with perfect linear separability while the detection direction sits nearly
orthogonal to the direction that controls the behavior, across four models and
surviving instruction tuning. Their measurement is geometric, an angle between
two directions on one behavior. Ours is behavioral and comparative, sweeping an
intervention across ten concepts and three models. Our contribution is not that
gap but the finding that the standard way of measuring the second quantity does
not measure what it is taken to.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Definitions}

\textbf{Readability} is the test AUROC of an $\ell_2$-regularized logistic probe
trained on residual-stream activations at the best layer, evaluated on held-out
\emph{template families} rather than held-out examples, and reported alongside a
shuffled-label control probe so selectivity is explicit
\citep{hewitt2019control}. Evaluating on held-out families rather than held-out
examples is intended to penalize a probe that relies on family-specific surface
cues.

\textbf{Controllability} is the area under the steering dose-response curve up to
a fluency ceiling. We form the steering direction as the difference of class
means, add $\alpha\,\hat v$ at the intervention layer, and sweep $\alpha$ in
units of the layer's residual-stream RMS norm so that a coefficient means the
same thing across layers and models. At each strength we score the behavior with
a fixed judge and measure the perplexity of the output under the unsteered
model. The reported quantity is the mean absolute shift in behavior from baseline,
integrated over usable strengths. We use an integrated measure rather than a
single-point effect size because the latter is sensitive to the choice of
strength.

\textbf{Directional controllability} is the same integral without the absolute
value, over $\mathrm{sign}(\alpha)\,(b(\alpha) - b_0)$ for judged behavior
$b(\alpha)$ and unsteered baseline $b_0$. It is positive only when positive
coefficients raise the target behavior and negative ones lower it, so it credits
an intervention for moving the model the way the direction was built to move it.
Absolute controllability remains the preregistered primary measure and carries
every preregistered threshold; Section~\ref{sec:directional} is what happens when
the two disagree.

\textbf{Ground-truth concepts.} A signed summary that returns nothing is
ambiguous between an intervention with no directional effect and a summary
that cannot see one, and no amount of re-reading the same scores separates
them. We therefore construct four concepts on which the judge is absent and
the answer is known. Their behavioral readout is computed from the generated
string by a rule -- word count, the fraction of letters capitalized, the
fraction of non-space characters that are digits, and the fraction of words
that are French function words -- each mapped to $[0,1]$ so the same
dose-response machinery applies unchanged. Because the readout is a rule, the
direction a positive coefficient should move it in follows from construction
rather than from an annotator, and a signed area can be scored correct or
incorrect rather than merely reported.

They are surface-confounded on purpose: length is length and capitals are
capitals. That is the price of decidability and not a defect here, because what
is being tested is the instrument rather than representation geometry, and
nothing from them bears on whether ordinary concepts are steerable. They carry
thirty evaluation prompts rather than six and use an extended coefficient grid
(Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\textbf{Fluency ceiling.} A strength is unusable if steered-output perplexity
under the unsteered model exceeds twice the unsteered baseline, or degenerate
repetition appears. The threshold is fixed in advance, so the largest usable
strength follows a stated rule rather than inspection of the results.

\subsection{Why the two quantities can differ}

A probe at layer $L$ asks about the \emph{encoder}: is the concept written
linearly separably into the residual stream by that depth? Steering asks about
the \emph{decoder}: is the computation of layers $L{+}1$ onward sensitive to
movement along it? To first order the behavior change is
$\alpha \lVert \nabla f(h) \rVert \cos(\hat v, \nabla f(h))$, so controllability
depends on how much of the unit direction $\hat v$ lies in the subspace the
downstream computation reads, while readability follows a Fisher-style ratio of
between-class to within-class variance along $\hat v$. A concept can separate its
classes cleanly along a direction later layers barely read.

\subsection{Terminology}

``Unsteerable under tested interventions'' refers to the interventions applied
here; ``immovable'' is a stronger claim about the concept. A concept enters the
danger zone only if it clears both raw thresholds (readability AUROC $\geq 0.9$,
controllability area $\leq 0.05$) with intervals excluding the thresholds, and is
called immovable only if it then resists a six-intervention gauntlet:
single-layer addition, multi-layer addition, clamping and directional ablation on
the difference-of-means direction, plus single-layer addition on the probe-weight
and RepE reading-vector directions (Appendix~\ref{app:gauntlet}). Even a
confirmed pass is bounded by those six and does not establish that no
intervention exists.

\subsection{The primary geometric hypothesis (H1)}

The most direct restatement of the asymmetry is mechanism M1, read/write
mismatch: a concept is a spectator feature to the extent it is written into a
subspace the output distribution barely depends on. This predicts
controllability rises with \emph{output overlap}, the fraction of the concept
direction lying in the top-$k$ right singular subspace of the unembedding matrix,
which is measurable from weights alone with no extra forward passes. We fix
$k=512$ in advance for every model (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}) and did not sweep
it, so the test is conditional on that choice. H1 is the
preregistered primary test: partial Spearman of output overlap against
controllability, conditioning on readability, with a cluster bootstrap over
concepts \citep{cameron2008bootstrap}, since the 30 retained points are three
observations of each of ten concepts and are not independent.

\subsection{Judge and reproducibility}

Behavior is scored by a model rather than by humans at scale, a practice with
known position, verbosity and self-preference biases \citep{zheng2023judge}. We
use a fixed logit judge (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, read from the Yes/No token
mass), held identical across every target model so that cross-model
comparison is valid, and validate it against a human rater in the Limitations. A degenerate judge (one score across a whole
sweep) produces a flat dose-response curve, which is the pattern the danger-zone
criterion selects for; such points are detected and excluded. The concept datasets,
probes, steering harness with all robustness variants, and the full gap-map data
are released. Full protocol and hyperparameters are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\section{Experimental setup}

\textbf{Models.} Four instruction-tuned families spanning 7B to 9B parameters were
run: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and
Gemma-2-9b-it, all in 4-bit for inference on a single 16GB GPU. Gemma-2 is
loaded with eager attention because its attention soft-cap is ignored by the
fused kernel.

Gemma-2-9b-it is withheld under the positive control described below, so
judge-scored results are the remaining three models. It is \emph{retained}
throughout the ground-truth analysis, which uses no judge and therefore has no
control to fail; the forty ground-truth points are all four models. Its
judge-scored numbers appear in Appendix~\ref{app:full}.

\textbf{Concepts.} Ten concepts spanning easy-to-steer to hard-to-steer:
sentiment (the positive control), certainty, factuality, formality, honesty,
refusal, rudeness, sycophancy \citep{sharma2024sycophancy}, topic (science vs
everyday), and verbosity. Each is built as minimal pairs tagged by template
family.

\textbf{Surface audit.} Before extraction, a leave-one-family-out TF-IDF audit
checks that no concept can be separated on lexical cues alone. Eight concepts
pass (Appendix~\ref{app:concepts}); two (topic and verbosity) are declared
surface-confounded in advance and
reported with that caveat rather than dropped (Appendix~\ref{app:stimuli} gives
example stimuli for every concept), because domain membership and
length are surface properties by construction.

\label{sec:setup_control}
\textbf{Positive control (P9).} Steering must demonstrably work on a model
before that model's controllability numbers are trusted. A model whose sentiment
control falls below an absolute floor is withheld entirely, because a broken
harness puts every concept in the danger zone and is indistinguishable from a
discovery.

On the absolute metric all four models clear the floor (sentiment
controllability $0.215$ Qwen, $0.163$ Mistral, $0.101$ Llama, $0.110$ Gemma;
floor $0.10$). Read directionally, and measured on thirty evaluation prompts
with intervals for the mean, the control is passed by three. Signed sentiment
area resolves positive on Mistral ($+0.235$, CI $[+0.186, +0.283]$), Qwen
($+0.188$, CI $[+0.147, +0.230]$) and Llama ($+0.097$, CI $[+0.041, +0.153]$),
and does not resolve on Gemma ($+0.025$, CI $[-0.029, +0.080]$). We withhold
Gemma and retain the other three.

This is the strict form of the control, requiring that it \emph{demonstrate}
steering rather than merely fail to contradict it, and we can now apply it
because three models satisfy it. An earlier version of this work could not: on
six prompts Llama's control was unresolved at $+0.083$ and we retained it on a
judgment call about an unresolved sign, while withholding Gemma on the grounds
that its estimate was negative. Neither premise holds now. Llama resolves, and
Gemma's estimate is positive rather than negative ($+0.025$); it is withheld
because it does not demonstrate steering, which is the criterion we should have
been applying throughout.

We also withdraw a claim built on the earlier withholding. We previously
reported that Gemma's ground-truth controllability sat five to eight times below
the other three, and read it as that model withholding its concepts. It does not
replicate: under the four-layer band Gemma's median was $0.0040$ against
$0.0195$--$0.0342$, but banded controllability is a maximum over four layers, and
at a single layer on the same four concepts Gemma's median is above Llama's and
Qwen's. Across all ten concepts a Kruskal--Wallis test on absolute area does not
separate models at all ($H=0.33$, $p=0.95$). The gap was the other models
gaining more from that maximum than Gemma did. Table~\ref{tab:withhold} reports the
primary test under all three samples. The estimate moves from $-0.451$ to
$-0.450$ to $-0.430$, so the conclusion does not depend on where this line is
drawn; only the four-model interval excludes zero, and that is the sample whose
control we no longer trust.

Two things count against us here. Llama clears the floor by $0.001$, and applying
interval exclusion to the floor as we do to the danger-zone thresholds would
withhold Llama and Mistral too, leaving nothing. And a model failing
directionally while passing on the absolute metric is the paper's own
observation, arriving in the control rather than the results.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The check recovers a direction that is known in advance}
\label{sec:groundtruth}

Figure~\ref{fig:groundtruth} and Table~\ref{tab:groundtruth} give the forty
ground-truth points, ten concepts on four models, swept on a common grid with no
judge anywhere in the measurement; Appendix~\ref{app:groundtruth} gives every
point with its interval and arm split.

Twenty-six of the forty have a signed area whose interval excludes zero, and 25
of those are positive: the direction the concept was constructed to be steered
in. \texttt{gt\_common}, \texttt{gt\_longwords} and \texttt{gt\_lowercase} resolve
on all four models, \texttt{gt\_french}, \texttt{gt\_uppercase} and
\texttt{gt\_firstperson} on three. Where the sign resolves it is almost always
the sign construction predicts.

\textbf{The exception, reported rather than absorbed.} One point resolves
against its construction: \texttt{gt\_question} on Llama-3.1-8B, signed area
$-0.024$ with interval $[-0.037, -0.010]$. It is not a borderline interval
scraping past zero. The dose-response is monotone in the wrong direction at
Spearman $-0.96$, so the model reliably produces \emph{fewer} question marks as
the question direction is added. We have no mechanistic account of it, and an
earlier version of this paper claimed that nothing anywhere resolved against its
construction, which was true of a smaller concept set and is not true of this
one. A check whose value is that it can return an unwelcome answer has to be
allowed to return one.

\textbf{Headroom does not explain which points resolve.} The obvious objection
to any validation like this is that the concepts which resolve are simply the
ones with room to move, in which case the check is measuring available range
rather than steerability. The concept set is built to test that directly:
unsteered baselines span $0.00$ to $0.98$, so baseline position varies
independently of concept identity. It does not survive. Whether a sign resolves
is unrelated to headroom (Spearman $+0.01$, $p=0.97$; mean headroom $0.62$ among
resolved points against $0.78$ among unresolved). The room available to an
absolute deviation, $\max(b, 1-b)$ for baseline $b$, correlates
\emph{negatively} with the measured effect (Spearman $-0.52$, $p=0.001$), which
is the reverse of what a ceiling artifact predicts. A Kruskal--Wallis test on
absolute area separates concepts ($H=21.5$, $p=0.010$) and not models ($H=0.33$,
$p=0.95$).

The cleanest version is a matched pair. \texttt{gt\_lowercase} and
\texttt{gt\_uppercase} read the same surface property from opposite ends, so they
have identical room available to an absolute deviation, $0.97$, differing only
in which side of baseline that room is on. Their absolute areas agree closely
within three of four models (Llama $0.0058$ against $0.0044$, Qwen $0.0030$
against $0.0031$, Mistral $0.0254$ against $0.0205$) despite baselines $0.94$
apart. On Gemma they diverge by a factor of ten ($0.0415$ against $0.0043$),
which is a concept-by-model interaction we cannot attribute to headroom either,
since the room is matched there too. We show it rather than average it away.

\textbf{Where it does not resolve.} \texttt{gt\_punctuation} resolves nowhere and
\texttt{gt\_length} on one model only. \texttt{gt\_digits} resolves on two, with
effects so small ($+0.001$) that they are statistically but not practically
distinguishable from zero: the behavior largely appears past the fluency
ceiling, so the usable range ends before it starts.

The importance of this section is what it licenses rather than what it shows. A
directional summary that never resolved anything would be indistinguishable from
a broken one, and every null elsewhere in the paper would be uninterpretable.
Because the check resolves the correct sign on most points where the correct
sign is knowable, and does so consistently across models, a null on a
judge-scored concept is a statement about that concept or that judge rather than
about the metric.

\subsection{The absolute summary diverges from the directional one, and the
difference is the judge}
\label{sec:judgedep}

differently. Over the retained thirty points the median share of measured
effect attributable to directional movement is $0.387$, and the two summaries

non-trivial effect it is $1.000$ (Figure~\ref{fig:groundtruth}b). Removing
the judge removes the disagreement.

$0.007$ to $0.024$ there and $0.118$ to $0.260$ under the larger, against a
$0.05$ threshold, so the criterion selects it on every model under one judge
and on none under the other.

\subsection{What the directional summary can and cannot resolve}
\label{sec:directional}

\textbf{The signed summary resolves a judge-scored direction far less often.}
On the four concepts re-measured with corrected intervals, on the same four
models and the same grid as the ground-truth set, 7 of 16 points have a sign
distinguishable from zero against 26 of 40 for the rule readout: $44\%$ against
$65\%$. Five of the seven are positive. Appendix~\ref{app:signed} gives the
per-point intervals and arm splits.

\textbf{The gap is not a sample-size artifact, and we can show it.} The
judge-scored concepts originally carried six evaluation prompts against the
ground-truth set's thirty, so the comparison confounded the readout with the
sample. Repeating the judge-scored measurement at thirty prompts, holding the
judge, the grid and the models fixed, settles it. The intervals tighten roughly
as sampling predicts, mean width $0.175$ to $0.099$ for a ratio of $0.57$ where
$1/\sqrt{5} = 0.45$ is the pure-sampling expectation. The point estimates do
not survive the enlargement: their median magnitude falls by $0.028$ and 7 of 16
signs flip. \texttt{refusal} on Gemma moves from $+0.139$ to $+0.015$ and
\texttt{topic\_science} on Mistral from $-0.201$ to $+0.043$. The six-prompt
estimates were not merely uncertain; most of them were not measuring anything.

\textbf{A correction to our own instrument, and what it cost.} An earlier
version of this work reported that 3 of 30 judge-scored points resolved, and
took the failure of those intervals to tighten with more prompts as evidence
about the judge. Both were artifacts of our own code. The per-coefficient
interval we propagated was a percentile of the individual generation scores
rather than a confidence interval for their mean: about three times too wide on
a judge readout at thirty prompts, and degenerate on a sparse rule readout,
where more than $97.5\%$ of generations score zero and both percentiles collapse
onto it. A percentile interval also cannot narrow as the sample grows, so the
prompt-count comparison was uninformative by construction rather than
informative about judges. Recomputing from per-generation scores moved the
ground-truth count from 6 of 40 to 26 of 40 on identical data. We report this
because the direction of the error flattered our own headline: the broken
interval made directional resolution look rarer than it is, which is the
conclusion the paper was arguing for. All runs now persist per-generation
scores so any interval can be recomputed without re-running a model.

\textbf{The effect is present where the judge is validated.} The resolved
points are not distributed at random across concepts. Several of them are
\texttt{sentiment}, the one concept on which the judge tracks a human closely
($\alpha = 0.76$ against $0.33$ pooled within concept), and on all three models
its dose-response monotonicity is positive ($+0.98$, $+0.79$, $+0.50$). Where
the instrument demonstrably works, steering moves the model the intended way.
That is either the finding or the confound, and this study cannot tell which.
It is the reason Section~\ref{sec:limitations} treats judge validation as the
limiting factor rather than as one caveat among several.

\textbf{Signed area assumes a shape these curves do not have.} The summary
credits a response that rises with positive coefficients and falls with
negative ones, so it is mis-specified for a response that is symmetric or
saturating about the baseline. Splitting it by arm shows this is the common
case: 24 of 30 points respond on one side of the baseline only, and just two
respond correctly on both. The positive control is among the one-sided ones. On
Qwen, \texttt{sentiment} scores $+0.42$ on the negative arm and $-0.01$ on the
positive, because the unsteered baseline already sits near the top of the
range and there is no headroom to push into. A near-zero signed area therefore
does not distinguish an absent directional effect from a real one that is not
monotone through the baseline, and the arm columns in
Table~\ref{tab:signed} are what separate them.

\textbf{A sign-convention error would look like this and does not explain it.}
\texttt{certainty} responds negatively on every model, which is what an inverted
direction would produce, but its stimuli are correctly signed and a systematic
polarity error would show many concepts agreeing across models rather than the
two of ten chance predicts. With intervals attached the question dissolves:
\texttt{certainty} is unresolved everywhere, so there is no effect to attribute
to a bug.

None of this retracts the absolute measurements, which are what they are. It
bounds what may be concluded from them, and Section~\ref{sec:inverted} is the
worked case.

\subsection{A geometric result that depends on the metric}
\label{sec:inverted}

H1 predicts that controllability increases with output overlap. Measured with the
absolute metric the partial Spearman of output overlap against controllability,
conditioning on readability, is $-0.45$ with a cluster-bootstrap 95\% CI of
$[-0.75, 0.04]$: the estimate points the opposite way to the prediction, and the
interval contains zero. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} plots the relationship. On the
full four-model sample before the positive control withheld Gemma the same
interval was $[-0.72, -0.04]$ and did exclude zero, which is worth stating
plainly: the significance of this result did not survive applying our own
preregistered control.

We report the rest of the analysis because the sign is stable and because what
removes it is instructive. Two checks a reader would reasonably demand both pass.

\textbf{The fluency ceiling does not produce it.} A lexical direction might break
fluency early, truncating the coefficient range and shrinking an integrated area
mechanically. It does not happen here: output overlap is unrelated to where the
ceiling falls (Spearman $-0.10$, $p=0.61$), 27 of 30 sweeps reach the end of the
grid unbroken, conditioning the test on the ceiling leaves the estimate
unchanged, and \texttt{topic\_science} completes all nine coefficients on two of
three models with a flat rather than truncated curve
(Appendix~\ref{app:controls}).

\textbf{No single concept produces it.} \texttt{topic\_science} holds the highest
overlap and lowest controllability on every model, so it is the natural suspect.
Leave-one-concept-out keeps the estimate negative in all ten refits, from $-0.22$
to $-0.55$; dropping \texttt{topic\_science} gives the weakest, so it contributes
disproportionately without carrying the sign.

\textbf{The metric does produce it.} Recomputing the identical test on directional
controllability gives $+0.14$ with a CI of $[-0.27, 0.44]$. The sign reverses and
the relationship disappears. Since Section~\ref{sec:directional} shows the
absolute metric is uncorrelated with the directional one, the honest reading is
that H1 was tested against a quantity that does not track directional control,
and the inversion is a property of that quantity rather than of the
representations. A result can survive a ceiling control and a
leave-one-concept-out and still be an artifact of its own summary statistic.

Retaining the absolute-metric reading would require a mechanism, and the
available one is that output overlap indexes how lexical a concept is rather
than how much the downstream computation depends on it: domain vocabulary for
\texttt{topic\_science}, token count for \texttt{verbosity}. We state it as a
hypothesis and note it does not cover \texttt{refusal}, which has low overlap and
low controllability, and is better explained by redundant encoding
\citep{joad2026refusal, elhage2022superposition}. Two further geometric features
are null after Benjamini-Hochberg correction \citep{benjamini1995fdr} and a ridge
predictor over all of them has leave-one-out $R^2$ near zero
(Appendix~\ref{app:controls}). No geometric quantity we tested accounts for the
gap in the predicted direction.

\section{Discussion}

The recommendation is concrete and cheap. A steering evaluation should report a
signed dose-response alongside any absolute one, with intervals and split by
arm, and should treat their agreement as a precondition for interpreting the
magnitude. It costs nothing beyond what is already computed: the same curves,
the same judge calls, one absolute value removed. Section~\ref{sec:groundtruth}
is the reason to believe the signed version means something, because it recovers
the correct direction on concepts where the correct direction is known.

The second implication is about LLM-judge scoring rather than about steering. On
concepts whose readout is a rule, the absolute and directional summaries almost
coincide; on judge-scored concepts they do not, and re-scoring identical sweeps
with a different judge moves magnitudes by up to $26.8\times$ and flips 3 of 16
signs. Controllability reported without its judge is not a quantity another
paper can compare against, and a numeric threshold applied to it is not a
criterion another paper can reuse. Our own preregistered danger-zone criterion
selects one concept under one judge and none under another, which is why that
apparatus is now in Appendix~\ref{app:dangerzone} rather than in the results.

A third implication concerns what probes license, and it is narrower than the
version we first wrote. Among concepts this probe recovers essentially
perfectly, controllability varies by a factor of twenty, so a working probe is
not evidence of a control handle. The converse, that unreadable concepts are
uncontrollable, is not something this design can address, because it contains
almost no unreadable concepts.

None of this says that concept directions never control behavior. The existence
proofs in Section~\ref{sec:related} stand, and so does our own French result:
adding a direction estimated from sixty minimal pairs makes three of four models
write French, fluently, with an interval excluding zero. What the paper questions
is the inference from a measured effect size to a claim of control, when the
measurement does not separate the two and the instrument computing it is not
reported.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{The two sides of the headline comparison are not the same size.} The
rule-readout side is ten concepts on four models, forty points, all measured
with intervals for the mean and all with per-generation scores retained. The
judge side of that comparison is four concepts on four models, sixteen points.
Those four are the load-bearing ones and they are measured under identical
conditions, but $65\%$ against $44\%$ rests on forty points against sixteen and
the second figure carries correspondingly more sampling noise.

The paper's other ten judge-scored concepts were measured before we corrected
the per-coefficient interval, and their runs did not retain per-generation
scores, so their intervals cannot be recomputed without re-running the models.
Every quantity derived from those runs that does not depend on an interval is
unaffected and is reported: the signed areas themselves, the directional shares,
the arm splits, the judge-swap magnitudes and sign flips, and the geometric
test, whose controllability intervals come from a prompt-level bootstrap that
was always an interval for the mean. What we do not report is a resolution count
from those runs set against the corrected one, because that would compare an
interval about three times too wide with a correct one and would overstate the
gap this paper is about. Closing that properly means re-running ten concepts on
four models, which we have not done.

\textbf{The primary metric is the object of study.} Absolute controllability is
the preregistered measure and every preregistered threshold is applied to it, so
the danger zone, the gauntlet verdicts and the geometric test are all computed on
a quantity Section~\ref{sec:directional} shows does not track direction. We
report them because they were preregistered and because the disagreement between
the two metrics is the result, not because the absolute numbers should be read
as measures of control.

\textbf{Ten concepts, three models.} The cluster bootstrap resamples concepts,
and ten is few enough that inference over-rejects relative to its nominal size
\citep{cameron2008bootstrap}, so the intervals are indicative and the sign
carries more weight than the endpoints. Withholding a model cost a quarter of
the sample and moved the primary interval across zero. Adding concepts, not
models, is what would tighten this.

\textbf{The readability axis is saturated.} Twenty-four of thirty points sit at
AUROC $1.0$, because the construction that lets the surface audit pass also
makes concepts trivially separable, so that axis supports only a conditional
claim (Appendix~\ref{app:selectivity}).

\textbf{The judge bounds every judge-scored claim.} On those concepts an
unresolved directional effect and an uninformative judge predict the same
scores. Section~\ref{sec:groundtruth} is the reason this does not extend to
the paper's main claim, since it removes the judge rather than averaging it,
but it does not rescue the judge-scored magnitudes. Validation on more than one
concept is what would lift the condition.

\textbf{One annotator, and a judge that disagrees with them.} Within-concept
agreement between the human and the logit judge is Krippendorff's $\alpha$
\citep{krippendorff2019content} of $0.33$, against $0.18$ for a lexicon scorer,
and $0.76$ on the sentiment control. But on \texttt{refusal} the human scored
all ten sampled outputs identically while the judge spread them over $0.16$, and
the same pattern holds for \texttt{honesty}, \texttt{certainty} and
\texttt{rudeness}. Because noise in the behavior score adds apparent movement,
controllability on those four concepts is more likely an over-estimate than an
under-estimate. The labels come from one annotator who was blind to condition
but not to the hypothesis. A second annotator and a judge of comparable scale to
the target models are the two things this study most needs and does not have;
Appendix~\ref{app:limits} gives the detail.

\textbf{Nothing clean occupies the danger zone.} The only gauntlet-confirmed
occupant is \texttt{topic\_science}, declared surface-confounded in advance, and
the strongest non-confounded candidate, \texttt{refusal}, misses the criterion on
an interval the judge noise above inflates.

\textbf{One feature basis, one scale.} Directions come from difference of means,
probe weights and reading vectors, all assuming the concept is a direction
\citep{park2024linear}; sparse autoencoders \citep{cunningham2024sae} or
unsupervised methods \citep{burns2023ccs} could recover a movable feature where
these do not. All models are instruction-tuned and under 10B parameters, with
templated stimuli and six evaluation prompts per concept.

\section{Conclusion}

Steering is usually scored by how far a model moves, which cannot distinguish
moving a model in the intended direction from perturbing it. The repair is to
integrate the signed deviation instead, and the difficulty is that the repair
cannot validate itself. We validate it on ten concepts whose readout is a rule
over the generated string, where the correct answer follows from construction
and whose baselines span the unit interval so that headroom is separated from
concept identity: across four models, 26 of 40 points resolve and 25 of those
are positive, with three concepts resolving on all four models. One point
resolves against its construction and we report it as such.

With the check established, the same measurement separates instrument from
intervention. Where a rule computes the readout the absolute and signed
summaries almost coincide, median share $1.000$; where an LLM judge computes it
the median is $0.387$ and the two are uncorrelated. On matched models, grid and
prompt count the rule readout resolves $65\%$ of its points and the judge
$44\%$, and enlarging the judge's evaluation set fivefold tightens its
intervals as sampling predicts while collapsing its point estimates and flipping
7 of 16 signs. Re-scoring identical sweeps under a second judge moves magnitudes
by up to $26.8\times$ and flips 3 of 16 signs, and a preregistered criterion of
ours selects one concept under one judge and none under the other. The
divergence the paper began by documenting belongs to the judge, not to
steering.

The cost to a substantive conclusion is visible in our own preregistered
geometric hypothesis, which is inverted under the absolute summary, survives a
fluency-ceiling control and leave-one-concept-out, and disappears under the
signed one. A result can pass the diagnostics the field asks for and still be a
property of the statistic underneath it. Report the signed dose-response beside
the absolute one, with intervals and split by arm, and say which judge produced
it.

\section{Concept construction and the surface audit}
\label{app:concepts}

Each concept is constructed as minimal pairs grouped into template families, and
probes are evaluated on held-out families rather than held-out examples. Two
build-time invariants restrict the contrast from being carried by surface
vocabulary: marker words are disjoint across families, and paired markers are
length-matched.

Before extracting activations we run a leave-one-family-out TF-IDF audit that
tries to separate the classes from surface form alone, scored two-sided so a
classifier that ranks a held-out family in reverse order is still treated as
having found a lexical contrast. Eight of ten concepts score exactly $0.500$.
This is the floor of the statistic rather than a strong result: because marker
vocabulary is disjoint by construction, a model trained on five families has no
feature that fires on the sixth. The audit therefore verifies that the
construction succeeded and is not independent evidence that the probe reads
concept meaning. During development it detected duplicated families, markers
shared across families, and a length imbalance that separated families at AUROC
$0.94$ with no marker word transferring.

Two concepts fail the audit and are declared surface-confounded in advance
rather than excused. \texttt{verbosity} is length, which is a surface property by
definition, and scores $1.000$. \texttt{topic\_science} is domain membership,
carried by content vocabulary, and scores $0.833$. Both are reported with the
confound stated. This bears on the main result, since the two gauntlet-confirmed
danger-zone points are \texttt{topic\_science}.

\section{Protocol and hyperparameters}
\label{app:hyper}

\textbf{Models.} Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9b-it, all loaded in 4-bit for inference on a
single 16GB accelerator. Gemma-2 is loaded with eager attention because its
attention soft-cap is not applied by the fused kernel.

\textbf{Probes.} $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression, $C=1.0$, averaged over
three seeds. The layer is selected on a validation family split disjoint from
both train and test, never on test AUROC, with ties broken toward the middle of
the network. Readability is the test AUROC at the selected layer, with a
$1000$-sample bootstrap interval, reported beside a shuffled-label control
probe.

\textbf{Steering.} Directions are difference-of-means unless stated. The
coefficient grid is $\{-3, -2, -1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3\}$ in units of the
residual-stream RMS norm at the intervention layer, so a coefficient means the
same thing across layers and models. The intervention spans a band of four
layers centered on the probe layer, clipped at the ends of the network; two of
the 30 retained points are clipped this way (\texttt{formality} selects layer 0
and \texttt{sycophancy} layer 2 on one model each), so their band is narrower
than a mid-network point's and not strictly comparable to it.

\textbf{What steering is measured on.} The dose-response is evaluated on each
concept's \texttt{eval\_prompts}, a set of open-ended generation prompts that is
disjoint by construction from the minimal pairs the direction is estimated from:
the direction comes from the pairs, the effect is measured on the prompts, and no
text appears in both. The steering result is therefore not evaluated on the
templates it was fitted to. The set is small, six prompts per concept, which
bounds the resolution of every controllability number in the paper and is the
cheapest thing to enlarge in a rerun.

\textbf{Output overlap.} We take the top $k=512$ right singular vectors of the
unembedding matrix by randomized SVD, computed once per model and cached, and
report the Euclidean norm of the unit-normalized concept direction's projection
onto that subspace, a quantity in $[0,1]$. The value $512$ was fixed in advance
as a tractable stand-in for the full vocabulary, not tuned: a full decomposition
of a $152{,}000 \times 3584$ head per concept was out of budget. We report the
primary test at this $k$ only, and its sensitivity to $k$ is untested, which is a
real gap given that the test's sign is the paper's claim about it. Note that the
\texttt{n\_directions} feature in the released results is a different quantity,
the effective dimensionality of the per-family direction set, and is not $k$. A strength is unusable if steered-output
perplexity under the unsteered model exceeds twice the unsteered baseline, or
if degenerate repetition appears. The threshold is fixed in advance.

\textbf{Gauntlet.} Single-layer addition, multi-layer addition, clamping and
directional ablation on the difference-of-means direction, plus single-layer
addition on the probe-weight and RepE reading-vector directions. The gauntlet is
run only on concepts that the default intervention failed to move; a concept
that never entered the gauntlet is recorded as such rather than as having failed
it.

\textbf{Thresholds.} A point enters the danger zone at raw readability
$\geq 0.9$ and raw controllability $\leq 0.05$, in both cases with the bootstrap
interval excluding the threshold. The positive control floor is a
dose-response area of $0.10$.

\textbf{Judge.} A single fixed judge, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct in fp16, scored
from the Yes/No token mass in a single forward pass rather than by parsing
generated text. The judge is held identical across all four target models, since
danger-zone membership is decided on raw controllability and a judge that varied
with the condition would make those values incomparable. Each result record
stores the judge identity and a flag indicating whether the model under study
scored its own outputs.

\textbf{Compute.} The full study is roughly ten accelerator-hours on two T4s,
dominated by the steering sweeps. The judge validation adds about ninety
seconds.

\section{The saturated axis, and selectivity as an alternative}
\label{app:selectivity}
\label{app:saturation}

Readability is the preregistered $x$-axis and it is saturated: 24 of 30 points
sit at AUROC $1.0$, so the axis carries almost no variance and the headline
correlation is uninformative rather than a clean null. The cause is the
concept construction itself. Minimal pairs whose marker vocabulary is disjoint
across template families are what makes the surface audit pass, and they also
make every concept trivially separable in activation space.

Selectivity, the probe AUROC minus its shuffled-label control, spreads the same
points across roughly half the unit interval (Figure~\ref{fig:selectivity}).
We report it as a secondary axis rather than substituting it for the
preregistered one. It is not a drop-in replacement: selectivity mixes how
readable a concept is with how much structure the shuffled-label control
happens to find, so a low value can mean either a weak probe or a strong
control, and the two have different implications. A study designed around this
axis from the start would need concepts built to vary in readability, which
ours were not.

\section{The robustness battery, per intervention}
\label{app:gauntlet}

battery, with the dose-response area each of the six interventions achieved.
Twelve of the thirty retained points entered it; the rest were already moved by
the default intervention and never reached it.

The table is the evidence behind the terminology distinction. Six points resist
all six interventions and are called immovable. Six are moved by at least one,
and the column showing which one is what separates those two claims:
\texttt{formality} on Qwen and Llama, \texttt{verbosity} on Qwen, and
\texttt{refusal} on Qwen each yield to a specific intervention while resisting
the default one, which is exactly the case a single-intervention protocol would

The battery inherits the limitation of Section~\ref{sec:directional}. Each cell
is an absolute dose-response area, so a point called immovable is one that no
intervention perturbed by more than the threshold, which is a stronger and
cleaner statement than the same threshold applied to a moved point. Where a
verdict rests on a margin of a few thousandths, as \texttt{verbosity} on Llama
($0.047$) and \texttt{refusal} on Mistral ($0.048$) do against a $0.05$
threshold, the categorical verdict is carrying more weight than the measurement
supports and should be read as a boundary case.

\section{The danger zone and the robustness battery}
\label{app:dangerzone}

This material was the main result of an earlier version of this paper and is
reported here rather than in the main text for two reasons. Its criterion and
its verdicts are computed on absolute controllability, the summary
Section~\ref{sec:judgedep} shows is judge-dependent, and its only confirmed
occupant does not survive a change of judge: \texttt{topic\_science}
clears the $0.05$ threshold on every model under the larger judge
(Table~\ref{tab:rejudge}). The terminology distinction it introduces, between
``unsteerable under tested interventions'' and ``immovable'', survives that and
is used throughout.


Figure~\ref{fig:dose} shows representative dose-response curves. The danger
zone has one confirmed occupant, \texttt{topic\_science} on Mistral,
immovable against all six interventions. That concept is the least controllable
on all three retained models ($0.024$, $0.019$, $0.011$) and survives the
gauntlet on all three; on Qwen and Llama it stays outside the zone only because
its readability interval falls below $0.9$. It is also one of the two concepts
declared surface-confounded in advance, so it is not a clean example of a
readable-but-immovable concept.

\texttt{refusal} is the clean candidate and it misses. Its controllability is
$0.047$, $0.038$ and $0.033$, but the upper bound of its interval exceeds the
$0.05$ threshold on all three models, so it stays outside the zone. It is not
surface-confounded, it is safety-relevant, and it survives the gauntlet on two of
three models. We report it as a boundary case, noting that the interval keeping
it out is inflated by the judge noise described in the Limitations.

\section{The readability axis, and what it can support}

Figure~\ref{fig:gapmap} is the gap map: every retained concept-model pair in the
readability-controllability plane, both axes min-max normalized within model to
remove cross-model scale differences. The Spearman correlation between the two
axes is $0.12$ with a 95\% bootstrap CI of $[-0.28, 0.58]$.

That number should not be read as a null. Readability is $1.00$ at 24 of the 30
points and falls below $0.90$ only three times, twice on \texttt{sycophancy}
probes that failed outright at $0.57$; excluding those three moves the
correlation to $+0.26$ ($n=27$, $p=0.20$). Neither value is distinguishable from
zero and neither can be: a predictor with almost no variance cannot be shown
unrelated to anything, so the correlation is uninformative rather than evidence
of independence. The
claim the data support is the conditional one: among concepts a probe recovers
essentially perfectly, controllability still ranges from $0.011$ to $0.215$, a
factor of twenty. High linear readability does not guarantee a control handle. It
is the converse, that low readability implies low controllability, that this
design cannot address, because it contains almost no unreadable concepts.

Appendix~\ref{app:saturation} reports the same correlation against probe
selectivity, which does vary, and finds nothing there either.
Appendix~\ref{app:full}
adds confidence intervals, selectivity, the shuffled-label control probe, the
selected layer and the robustness-battery outcome for every point.

\section{Absolute and directional controllability, per point}
\label{app:directional}

Table~\ref{tab:directional} gives both summaries of every retained dose-response
curve, with the monotonicity of the curve alongside. The two summaries are
computed from identical data by identical code differing only in an absolute

but real directional effect, the sign of the monotonicity would agree across

agreement well above chance.

\section{Directional controllability: intervals and arms}
\label{app:signed}

Per-prompt behavior scores are not retained in the result files, but every
coefficient carries a prompt-level bootstrap interval. We propagate those into
the signed area by Monte Carlo, drawing a behavior value per coefficient from a
normal whose standard error is the interval half-width over $1.96$, clipping to
$[0,1]$ because the judge emits a probability, and recomputing the area $4000$
times. Draws are independent across coefficients while the real scores share a
prompt set and are positively correlated, and correlated errors partly cancel in
a difference from baseline, so these intervals are conservative. That is the
safe direction for a claim that a sign is resolved.

3 of the 30 points have a signed area whose interval excludes zero, and all
3 are positive. 16 points have a negative signed area and none of them is
resolved, which is why this paper makes no claim about wrong-way movement.

\textbf{Pos} and \textbf{Neg} are the same integral computed over one arm of
the sweep, each oriented so that a positive value means the model moved the way
the direction intended. A concept with a genuine bidirectional effect scores
positive on both. 2 of 30 points do; 24 respond on one arm only. A signed area
near zero is therefore ambiguous between no directional effect and an effect
that is real but not monotone through the baseline, and these two columns are
what distinguish them.

\section{Judge validation and annotation setup in full}
\label{app:limits}

Agreement must be read within concept rather than pooled. Pooled agreement over
a multi-concept sheet mostly reflects that refusal text scores low and honesty
text scores high, which is trivially true when they answer different questions;
controllability is a within-concept quantity. We hand-labeled 100 generations
stratified across all ten concepts and compared three raters: a human, the fixed
logit judge, and a lexicon scorer.

On the positive control the judge tracks the human closely ($\alpha = 0.76$ for
sentiment), so the instrument behaves as intended where behavior varies. On
\texttt{topic\_science} the judge's spread across sampled generations is $0.02$,
below the human's $0.05$, so the near-zero controllability that places this
concept in the danger zone is unlikely to be a judge artifact.

Two features of the annotation setup bear on how much weight the validation
carries. The labels come from a single annotator, so no inter-annotator
agreement can be computed and the reported figures rest on that annotator's
judgment; the ten repeated items give intra-rater reliability only. Against
that, the annotator was blind to condition: the labeling sheet carries the
generation, its concept and the scoring question, and does not record which
model produced the text or at what steering strength, so the labels cannot have
been shaped by knowing whether an item came from a steered or unsteered
condition. The annotator was not blind to the hypothesis under test, which a
second independent rater would address and which we did not do.

The exception is \texttt{refusal}. The human scored all ten sampled outputs
identically, while the judge spread them over $0.16$. That interval is the sole
reason \texttt{refusal} misses the danger-zone criterion. We record this rather
than argue from it, since determining where \texttt{refusal} lies would require
re-measurement with a better-validated judge.

A second judge cannot be run from the released artifacts alone. The result files
cache two generations per coefficient per point, 720 in total, which is enough
to inspect behavior but not to re-score the curves. Re-judging requires
regenerating, which is the main cost of the experiment.

Section~\ref{sec:directional} rules out judge noise as the explanation for the
directional result specifically: wrong-way movement appears at the same rate in
the four concepts with near-zero human variance as in the six without. That
argument concerns the sign of the effect and does not rehabilitate the
magnitudes on those four concepts.

\section{Failure modes encountered, and how they were caught}
\label{app:failures}

Several measurement errors in this study would have produced a plausible-looking
result rather than an obvious crash. We record them because each one is a
failure mode a replication could repeat, and because the specific pattern is
instructive: in every case the corrupted quantity was pushed toward the value
the danger-zone criterion selects for, so the error would have manufactured
support for our own hypothesis.

\textbf{A judge returning a constant.} An early sweep produced controllability
exactly $0.000$ on the positive control with no parse failures. A judge that
emits the same score for every generation yields a perfectly flat dose-response
curve, which is indistinguishable from a concept that steering cannot move. The
harness now records whether a sweep returned a single distinct score and
excludes such points, because a warning is not sufficient when the number being
warned about is the number the finding is defined by.

\textbf{Prompting an instruction-tuned model without its chat template.} The
same early sweep fed raw instructions to instruct models, which continued the
instruction rather than following it. The resulting text was uniform enough
that the judge scored it identically throughout, again producing a flat curve.
Steering itself was never broken; the stimulus was.

\textbf{Applying danger-zone thresholds to normalized values.} Both axes are
min-max normalized within model for the correlation and the figure, which is
necessary because raw AUROC and raw dose-response area are not comparable across
models. Applying the danger-zone thresholds after that normalization is not:
min-max normalization guarantees that some point sits at each extreme, so
thresholding normalized values manufactures a danger-zone occupant out of pure
scatter. The thresholds are therefore applied to raw values, and a test
constructed from scatter confirms that no occupant appears.

\textbf{A fluency ceiling that admitted the point that breached it.} The
original ceiling walked outward by absolute coefficient against a single scalar,
so a breach at one sign could leave the opposite sign's breaching point inside
the usable set. The ceiling now marks each point on its own perplexity and
repetition and closes over each sign separately, and the confidence interval is
computed on the same usable set as the point estimate rather than on a magnitude
threshold.

\textbf{Selecting the probe layer on test AUROC.} Layer selection originally
maximized test AUROC and then reported that same maximum as readability. Layer
choice now happens on a validation family split disjoint from both train and
test.

\textbf{A verdict rule that ignored the sign of its own result.} The primary
test's reporting logic checked whether the estimate was in the predicted
direction only when its magnitude exceeded $0.7$. Our result is moderate
($-0.45$) with an interval excluding zero on the side opposite the prediction,
and it therefore fell through to a branch that described it as suggestive
support. A wrong-signed result whose interval excludes zero is evidence against
the hypothesis, and the reporting is now sign-aware at every magnitude.

\textbf{A hand-labelling sheet with no within-concept variance.} The first
judge-validation sheet was drawn as the first hundred generations in curve
order. Because steering frequently leaves output unchanged at adjacent
coefficients, 56\% of its rows were duplicates, four of ten concepts were absent
including the positive control, and the sampled generations spanned too little
of the sweep to exhibit within-concept variation. Agreement computed on it read
$+0.573$ pooled against $+0.022$ within concept. The sampler now deduplicates,
allocates across concepts round-robin, and spreads picks across the coefficient
range.

The common thread is that a metric can be wrong in a direction that flatters the
hypothesis without failing loudly. Every quantity reported here is covered by
tests that run the metric against synthetic data with a planted ground truth, so
a metric that returns a wrong number fails a test rather than reaching a figure;
the two reporting errors above were both found that way.
